Why Do We Light Diyas on Deepavali? Rama's Return to Ayodhya, Told for Children

Deepavali8 Nov 2026 · 4 min read

Every Deepavali, homes across India fill with small clay lamps. Children help to fill them with oil, set the wicks and line them up on doorsteps and walls. But why lamps? The best-known answer is a story that families have told for generations: the night Prince Rama came home to Ayodhya.

The story: a whole city waiting for one prince

In the Ramayana, Prince Rama of Ayodhya leaves home for fourteen years in the forest, keeping a promise his father King Dasharatha made. His wife Sita and his brother Lakshmana go with him. In the forest, Sita is carried away by Ravana, the king of Lanka. With the help of Hanuman and an army of vanaras, Rama crosses the sea, defeats Ravana and brings Sita back.

When the fourteen years are over, Rama, Sita and Lakshmana return to Ayodhya. In the Ramayana, the whole city comes out to welcome them, and his brother Bharata, who had ruled only as Rama's stand-in, hands the kingdom back to him. Tradition tells that the people of Ayodhya lit rows and rows of lamps so that the city glowed on the dark new-moon night. That is the picture Deepavali keeps alive: a dark night made bright because someone good came home.

What does the word "Deepavali" mean?

The name comes from Sanskrit: दीप (dīpa) means a lamp and आवलि (āvali) means a row. Deepavali is literally "a row of lamps". "Diwali" is the shorter form used in many parts of India.

One line that explains the festival

An ancient prayer from the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad asks for exactly what a diya does:

असतो मा सद्गमय। तमसो मा ज्योतिर्गमय। मृत्योर्मा अमृतं गमय॥

asato mā sad gamaya · tamaso mā jyotir gamaya · mṛtyor mā amṛtaṁ gamaya
Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 1.3.28

Meaning: "Lead me from the unreal to the real. Lead me from darkness to light. Lead me from death to immortality."

A single small flame does not argue with the darkness. It simply shines, and the darkness around it is gone. That is a lesson children understand at once.

More than one story

India is big, and Deepavali is told in more than one way. Many families tell all of these:

  • Rama's return to Ayodhya, the story in our video above.
  • Krishna and Satyabhama defeating Narakasura, remembered on Naraka Chaturdashi with an early-morning oil bath in many South Indian homes.
  • Goddess Lakshmi, who is worshipped on the new-moon night of Lakshmi Puja.
  • King Bali, remembered on Bali Padyami, especially in Karnataka.

The five days at a glance

  1. Dhanteras (Dhanatrayodashi): homes are cleaned and new vessels are bought.
  2. Naraka Chaturdashi: the victory over Narakasura; oil bath before sunrise in many homes.
  3. Lakshmi Puja (Amavasya): the main night of lamps and prayers.
  4. Govardhan Puja / Bali Padyami: Krishna lifting Govardhan Hill, and the remembrance of King Bali.
  5. Bhai Dooj (Bhau Beej, Yama Dwitiya): a day for brothers and sisters.

Customs and names change from region to region, and the exact dates follow the lunar calendar, so they move every year.

A five-minute diya activity for children

  1. Give each child one clay diya and let them decorate the outside with paint or rangoli colours.
  2. Ask: "Who would you light this lamp for?" A grandparent far away, a friend, a teacher.
  3. With an adult, place a little oil and a cotton wick in it.
  4. An adult lights it, and everyone says one kind wish for that person.
  5. Talk about it: one small lamp, one kind act, and the room is brighter.

Safety first: an adult should always light the lamp, keep it away from curtains and loose clothes, and never leave it burning alone.

FAQ

Is Deepavali the same as Diwali? Yes. Deepavali is the full Sanskrit name ("a row of lamps") and Diwali is the shorter form.

Why is Deepavali on a new-moon night? The main night of lamps, Lakshmi Puja, falls on the Amavasya (new moon) of the month of Kartika in the purnimanta calendar, Ashvina in the amanta calendar. It is the darkest night, which is why the lamps matter so much.
