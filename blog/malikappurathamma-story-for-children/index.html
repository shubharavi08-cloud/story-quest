Who Is Malikappurathamma? The Story of the Devi Who Waits at Sabarimala, Explained for Children

Songs22 Nov 2026 · 6 min read

Watch & take your step

When pilgrims finally reach the top of the hill at Sabarimala, climb the 18 holy steps and see Swami Ayyappa, many of them then walk a few minutes further to a smaller shrine close by. There, under the trees, sits a Devi with a gentle face. She is Malikappurathamma (ಮಾಳಿಗಪುರತ್ತಮ್ಮ), and her story is one of the most loved in the whole Ayyappa tradition. It is a story about a promise, a bundle of arrows, and a love that waits.

1. Where the story begins: after Mahishi fell

Yesterday we read how young Manikanta, the prince of Pandalam, defeated the demoness Mahishi in the forest near Erumeli. (If you missed it, read Petta Thullal at Erumeli.)

The tradition says the story did not end with her defeat. Mahishi had not always been a demoness. Long before, she had been a maiden named Leela, and a curse had turned her into the fierce buffalo-faced Mahishi. When Manikanta's arrow struck her, the curse broke. Out of the fallen body rose a maiden of light, gentle and shining, free at last.

Our song Belakina Kanye (the maiden of light) sings this moment in Kannada.

2. Manikanta's promise

The maiden bowed to Manikanta and thanked him for setting her free. Then she asked him to marry her.

Manikanta answered kindly but firmly. In his life at Sabarimala he would stay a brahmachari: a seeker who gives his whole heart to prayer and to his devotees. But he did not send her away. He made her a promise:

"Stay near me. I will marry you in the year when no kanni swami comes to see me."

A kanni swami is a pilgrim making the journey to Sabarimala for the very first time. And so the maiden agreed to wait, close to Ayyappa's own shrine. That is where she is worshipped today as Malikappurathamma, the mother of Malikappuram.

Our song Kaayuve Naanu (I will wait) is her answer, sung in Kannada.

3. The arrows of Sharamkuthi

Here is the clever, beautiful part of the story.

On the path up to the temple there is a spot called Sharamkuthi (ಶರಂಕುತ್ತಿ). Sharam means arrow, and kuthi means to pierce or plant. Every kanni swami carries an arrow on the journey, often bought at Erumeli, where the pilgrimage begins with dancing. At Sharamkuthi, the first-timers plant their arrows there, one after another.

The tradition says that each year Malikappurathamma comes in a procession to Sharamkuthi to see whether any arrows are there. And every year she finds them: hundreds and thousands of new arrows, from new pilgrims. So she knows that kanni swamis have come once again, and she returns to her shrine, still waiting, still blessing.

Our song Sharamkuthiya Baana (the arrow of Sharamkuthi) tells this part.

Children often ask: "So will she wait forever?" Devotees smile and say that as long as people keep coming to Ayyappa with fresh faith, the arrows will be there. In a way, every new pilgrim is part of the story.

4. Another telling: Poonkodi

In India, a beloved story is often told in more than one way, and this one is too. Our satsang also tells a second version. In it, Manikanta learnt the martial art of kalari at Cheerappanchira, and the daughter of his kalari guru, Poonkodi, came to love him and wished to be by his side. She too waits at Malikappuram.

Two tellings, one Devi. Both stories say the same thing in the end: the Devi beside Ayyappa is a picture of love that is patient, love that does not grab or demand, but waits and keeps blessing.

5. Visiting the Malikappuram shrine

Pilgrims who visit Malikappurathamma usually offer turmeric and kumkum, and many roll a coconut on the ground near the shrine as an offering. Young girls who go on the pilgrimage with their families are lovingly called Malikappurams, just as first-time pilgrims are called kanni swamis.

If your family is not travelling this year, you can still do what our satsang shows: a simple puja at home, with a little turmeric and kumkum before a picture of the Devi, and the song Malikappurathamma Sharanam.

A lesson for children: the strength of waiting

We usually think strong people are the ones who win fights, like Manikanta did. Malikappurathamma shows another kind of strength: waiting with a kind heart.

Talk about it with your child:

  • "Was there something I wanted right now, but it was better to wait for?"
  • "Can I wait for my turn on the swing without getting angry?"
  • "Can I keep being kind to someone, even when they cannot give me what I want?"

A promise kept, and a wish waited for with patience: that is the arrow every child can plant in their own heart.

FAQ

Is Malikappurathamma the same as Mahishi?

In the most popular telling, the Devi rose from Mahishi's body once the curse was broken, so she is Mahishi's true, gentle self, set free. In another telling she is Poonkodi, the daughter of Ayyappa's kalari guru. Families tell it the way their elders taught them.

Why do only kanni swamis plant arrows?

Because the promise is about them. Each first-timer's arrow tells the Devi that new devotees have come, so the wait continues. Each pilgrim group follows its own custom, so ask your Guruswami how yours does it.

Where is the Malikappuram shrine?

It is a short walk from the main Sabarimala temple, on the same hilltop, and most pilgrims visit it after seeing Ayyappa.
