This Will Pass: Kabir's Cold Pool and Bhagavad Gita 2.14 for Children

A Thought a Day16 Nov 2026 · 4 min read

Watch & take your step▶ हिंदी▶ ಕನ್ನಡ

Every child meets this moment sooner or later: something feels so uncomfortable that it seems it will last forever. A cold pool, a hard test, a sad evening, a scraped knee. One small swimming lesson, and one verse from the Bhagavad Gita, help a child see that feelings like these have a beginning and an end.

Kabir and the cold pool

Kabir has come for his first swimming lesson. The water looks blue and friendly, but when he dips one toe in, it is cold. He pulls his foot straight back.

"It's too cold. I can't get in."

His teacher stands on the step and holds out her hand. "Come and stand with me," she says, "and let's count to sixty together."

Kabir steps in. The cold bites at first. One, two, three... by twenty it is a little less sharp. By forty he notices the sun on the water. By sixty he has forgotten to shiver. The water did not change. Kabir simply stayed long enough for the feeling to pass.

This is a parable, a small story we use to explain the verse. But anyone who has stepped into a cold river, a sea, or a new classroom knows how true it is.

What Gita 2.14 says

In the second chapter of the Bhagavad Gita, Krishna tells Arjuna that the feelings our senses bring us are like visitors:

मात्रास्पर्शास्तु कौन्तेय शीतोष्णसुखदुःखदाः।
आगमापायिनोऽनित्यास्तांस्तितिक्षस्व भारत॥

mātrā-sparśās tu kaunteya śītoṣṇa-sukha-duḥkha-dāḥ · āgamāpāyino 'nityās tāṁs titikṣasva bhārata
Bhagavad Gita, Chapter 2, Verse 14

Meaning: "O son of Kunti, the touch of the senses with their objects brings cold and heat, pleasure and pain. They come and they go; they do not last. Bear them patiently, O Bharata."

Three words carry the verse:

  • śīta and uṣṇa, cold and heat: Krishna picks the simplest example, the kind Kabir felt in the pool.
  • āgamāpāyinaḥ, coming and going: every feeling arrives, and every feeling leaves.
  • titikṣasva, bear it patiently: not "pretend it isn't there", but "stay steady while it passes".

Notice that Krishna puts pleasure in the same list as pain. Happy moments also come and go. The verse is not sad; it is freeing. If nothing stays forever, then a bad feeling is never the end of the story.

Why it matters for children today

Children often feel that an uncomfortable moment is permanent:

  • "I'll never understand this sum." The confusion of the first ten minutes feels like forever.
  • "Everyone laughed at me, so it will always be like this." One bad day becomes a whole year in the mind.
  • "I'm bored, I need the phone now." A few minutes of boredom feel unbearable, so we rush to escape them.

Gita 2.14 gives children a simple tool: name the feeling, and remind yourself it is a visitor. Cold, nervous, sad, bored: it came, and it will go. Meanwhile, keep standing in the water.

This is not about ignoring real problems. If a child is hurt, unwell or unhappy for a long time, they need a grown-up's help. The verse is for the everyday discomforts that make us give up too early.

One small step for today

Today, when something feels hard, say quietly: "This will pass," and count slowly to sixty before you decide to quit.

Families can try it together: the first minutes of homework, a cold bath on a winter morning, waiting in a queue. At bedtime, ask each other: "What felt hard today, and how long did it really last?"

FAQ

Is the Kabir story from the Gita?

No. Kabir and the swimming pool are a simple parable we use to explain the verse. The teaching is Bhagavad Gita 2.14, spoken by Krishna to Arjuna on the battlefield of Kurukshetra.

What does "titikshasva" mean?

It means "bear it patiently" or "endure it calmly". The noun titikṣā is the quality of staying steady through heat and cold, pleasure and pain.

How can I explain this verse to a young child?

Use the pool. Ask: "Was the water still cold at sixty?" Then explain that sad, scared or bored feelings work the same way: they come, and they go.
