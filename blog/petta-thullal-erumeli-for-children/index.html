Petta Thullal at Erumeli: Why Ayyappa Pilgrims Dance With Colours, Explained for Children

Songs21 Nov 2026 · 6 min read

Watch & take your step

Picture a small town in the green hills of Kerala, full of drums. Hundreds of people walk past with their faces painted in bright colours, carrying sticks, toy arrows and bunches of leaves. They are not at a carnival. They are pilgrims on their way to Sabarimala, and they are dancing the Petta Thullal (ಪೇಟ್ಟ ತುಳ್ಳಲ್) at Erumeli (ಎರುಮೇಲಿ).

1. Where is Erumeli?

Erumeli is a town in the Kottayam district of Kerala, at the edge of the forests of the Western Ghats. For centuries it has been the starting point of the old forest path to Sabarimala, the Peruvazhi ("the great path"). From Erumeli the traditional route runs for over 40 km through forest, across rivers and over hills, to the Pamba river and then up to the temple.

Today most pilgrims travel by road to Pamba and climb only the last few kilometres. But many groups still stop at Erumeli first, because the pilgrimage is not only about reaching the temple. It is about the journey, and Erumeli is where the journey begins with joy.

2. The story: Manikanta and Mahishi

The tradition tells it like this. Long ago a demoness named Mahishi troubled the gods and the people. She had a boon that no one could defeat her except a child born of Shiva and Vishnu. That child was Manikanta, who grew up as the prince of Pandalam and is worshipped as Swami Ayyappa.

When young Manikanta went into the forest, he faced Mahishi and defeated her. Many people say the town's name remembers this: Erumeli is often linked to eruma, the Malayalam word for a buffalo, because Mahishi is described as a buffalo-faced demoness. (This is a popular telling of the name, not a settled fact of history.)

Our story songs tell this part in Kannada: Mahishiya Ahankara (Mahishi's pride) and Erumeliya Vijaya (the victory at Erumeli).

3. What is the Petta Thullal?

Thullal means a leaping, joyful dance. The Petta Thullal is the dance pilgrims perform at Erumeli before they set out for Sabarimala. It is said to remember the joy of the people after Mahishi was defeated, as if the whole town came out to celebrate Manikanta's victory.

Here is what you will see:

| What pilgrims do | What it is said to mean | |---|---| | Paint their faces with bright colours | Everyone looks the same: no rich, no poor, no "important" people | | Carry sticks, toy bows and arrows, and leaves | Like the hunters and villagers who went with Manikanta into the forest | | Dance to drums, chanting "Ayyappa thinthakathom, Swami thinthakathom" | Happiness and devotion, shared out loud | | Dance from one temple to another through the town | The journey starts together, as one group |

The dance moves from the small Petta Sastha temple (the Kochambalam) to the big Sastha temple of Erumeli (the Valiyambalam). In January, a few days before the Makaravilakku festival, two famous groups from Ambalappuzha and Alangad perform the grand Petta Thullal, and thousands of people gather to watch.

Our dance song for this moment is Petta Thullal Kunita. Play it loudly and dance along at home.

4. Vavar: the friend on the way

On the way between the two temples, the pilgrims stop at a special place: the shrine of Vavar, a mosque at Erumeli. The tradition remembers Vavar as a warrior who first fought Manikanta and then became his closest friend and helper. Pilgrims visit Vavar's shrine with respect before going on, and there is another shrine to Vavar, the Vavar Nada, near the temple at Sabarimala itself.

This is one of the most beautiful parts of the Sabarimala journey to share with children: the path to Swami Ayyappa passes through a friend's home of another faith. At the very start of the pilgrimage, the pilgrims honour friendship.

5. Why do grown-ups dance like children?

Ask a Guruswami and he may laugh and say: "Because at Erumeli we leave our pride behind." A judge, a farmer, a shopkeeper and a schoolboy all paint their faces, all hold leaves and sticks, all dance in the same street. Nobody is too old or too important to be silly with joy.

That is also the message of Mahishi's story. Mahishi was defeated because of her ahankara, her pride. The Petta Thullal is the opposite of pride: it is humble, happy and shared.

A lesson for children: leave one "pride" behind

Before the next festival, ask your child:

  • "Is there something I refuse to do because I think it is too babyish or not cool?"
  • "Can I play with my little cousin's game today, even if I think I am too big for it?"
  • "Can I say sorry first, even if I think I was right?"

Each of these is a small Petta Thullal: dropping a bit of pride to share a bit of joy.

FAQ

Is the Petta Thullal done only in January?

Pilgrim groups dance it at Erumeli all through the season, whenever they arrive to begin the forest walk. The grand Petta Thullal of the Ambalappuzha and Alangad groups happens in January, a few days before Makaravilakku.

Do children take part?

Yes. Kutti Swamis (children travelling with their families) often dance with the group, with painted faces and small toy arrows. Each group follows its own custom.

Who was Vavar?

In the tradition, Vavar was a warrior who became Ayyappa's friend and companion. Pilgrims honour him at his shrine at Erumeli and at Vavar Nada near the Sabarimala temple.
