Every child has had a day like this: too many sweets at a party, too much cricket in the sun, one more chapter of a comic long after lights-out. Nothing about laddoos, cricket or comics is bad. The trouble is the *amount*. One short story and one verse from the Bhagavad Gita explain the secret that grandparents have always known: **not too much, not too little, just right.**

## Aarav's week

On **Sunday**, Aarav went to a wedding. The laddoos were round, golden and full of cashews, so he ate one, then another, and then five more. Seven laddoos later, his tummy hurt so much that he could not enjoy the music.

On **Monday**, he played cricket with his friends until it was too dark to see the ball. He came home so tired that he fell asleep before dinner.

On **Tuesday**, he found a pile of new comics and read them under his blanket until midnight. In the morning he slept straight through the school bus horn.

That evening Grandpa sat down with a piece of paper and drew a big circle. He divided it into four parts and wrote a word in each: **food, play, study, sleep.** "Each of these is your friend," he said. "But each one needs its fair share of the circle. When one grows too big, it squeezes the others."

On **Wednesday**, Aarav tried it. Two laddoos, not seven. Cricket until the streetlights came on, then home. Homework, then one comic, then bed on time. On **Thursday** morning he woke up before the bus, fresh and happy.

Aarav, his grandpa and the laddoos are a simple parable we use to explain the verse. But you have probably seen a week like his at home.

## What Gita 6.17 says

In the sixth chapter of the Bhagavad Gita, Krishna teaches Arjuna about yoga, the steady and peaceful mind. Just before this verse, he says that yoga is not for the one who eats too much or too little, or who sleeps too much or too little. Then he gives the rule in one line:

<p lang="sa" style="font-size:1.25em;text-align:center;line-height:1.7;margin:18px 0 6px">युक्ताहारविहारस्य युक्तचेष्टस्य कर्मसु।<br>युक्तस्वप्नावबोधस्य योगो भवति दुःखहा॥</p>
<p style="text-align:center;color:var(--muted);font-size:.95em;margin:0 0 16px">yuktāhāra-vihārasya yukta-ceṣṭasya karmasu · yukta-svapnāvabodhasya yogo bhavati duḥkhahā<br>Bhagavad Gita, Chapter 6, Verse 17</p>

> **Meaning:** "For one who is balanced in eating and in play, balanced in effort at work, and balanced in sleep and in waking, yoga becomes the remover of all sorrow."

The word that keeps coming back is **yukta**, which means *fitting*, *joined well*, or simply *just right*. Krishna uses it four times in one line:

- **yukta-āhāra**, *balanced eating*: Aarav's two laddoos instead of seven.
- **yukta-vihāra**, *balanced play and rest*: cricket until the streetlights, then home.
- **yukta-ceṣṭa karmasu**, *balanced effort in work*: doing homework properly, without either skipping it or worrying over it all night.
- **yukta-svapna-avabodha**, *balanced sleep and waking*: going to bed on time, and getting up on time too.

And the reward is in the last word, **duḥkhahā**, *the one that destroys sorrow*. A tummy ache, a missed dinner, a missed bus: Aarav's three sorrows all came from one thing being too big.

## Why it matters for children today

Krishna's line was spoken thousands of years ago, but it fits a modern home surprisingly well:

- **Food:** festival season brings sweets every week. Enjoying them is part of the joy; the verse only asks us to stop before the joy turns into a tummy ache.
- **Play and screens:** play is one of the four parts of Grandpa's circle, not an enemy. The trouble starts when a game or a screen quietly eats into sleep and study.
- **Study:** "balanced effort" works in both directions. A child who studies only at the last minute and a child who is anxious and cannot stop are both out of balance.
- **Sleep:** the verse mentions sleep *and* waking. A regular bedtime and a regular wake-up time are a small daily form of yoga.

This is not about strict rules or a perfect timetable. It is about noticing when one part of the day has grown too big, and gently giving the others their share back.

## One small step for today

Today, **notice one thing you do too much, and give it just the right amount.** One less helping of sweets. Ten minutes less of the phone. Bed fifteen minutes earlier.

Families can draw Grandpa's circle together: a big circle on paper, four parts for **food, play, study and sleep**. Each person colours how big each part was today. Is one slice much bigger than the others? Choose one slice to make *just right* tomorrow, and check the circle again at the end of the week.

## FAQ

### Is the story of Aarav from the Gita?

No. Aarav, his grandpa and the seven laddoos are a simple parable we use to explain the verse. The teaching is Bhagavad Gita 6.17, spoken by Krishna to Arjuna in the sixth chapter, on the yoga of meditation.

### What does "yuktāhāra-vihārasya" mean?

It means "of one whose eating (āhāra) and recreation (vihāra) are balanced (yukta)". The rest of the verse adds balanced effort in work and balanced sleep and waking.

### How can I explain balance to a young child?

Use a plate or a pizza: "If one piece is huge, the others become tiny. A happy day gives every piece a fair share." Then draw the four-part circle together.
