Every parent, teacher and older sibling knows this feeling: you have said the same thing ten times, and nothing changes. Little ones do not copy what we *say*. They copy what we *do*. One short story about a pair of shoes, and one verse from the Bhagavad Gita, show why the quietest lesson is often the one that works.

## Riya and the row of shoes

Every evening, Mum says the same thing: *"Ishaan, put your shoes away!"* And every evening, Ishaan's shoes stay exactly where he kicked them off, right by the door.

His big sister Riya has an idea. She stops reminding him. Instead, she simply lines up her own shoes, neatly, by the wall, every single day. She waters the tulsi plant. She packs her school bag the night before. She smiles. No lectures at all.

One day passes. Then two. Then three. On the fourth morning, Mum opens the door and stops. There, beside Riya's shoes, are **two tiny shoes, lined up just the same way.**

Ishaan did not need to be told again. He only needed someone to show him.

This is a small parable, a story we use to explain the verse. But you have probably seen it at home: the toddler who "reads" a newspaper upside down because Grandpa does, or the child who folds her hands before eating because everyone at the table does.

## What Gita 3.21 says

In the third chapter of the Bhagavad Gita, Krishna explains to Arjuna why the way we act matters so much, not only for ourselves but for everyone who is watching:

<p lang="sa" style="font-size:1.25em;text-align:center;line-height:1.7;margin:18px 0 6px">यद्यदाचरति श्रेष्ठस्तत्तदेवेतरो जनः।<br>स यत्प्रमाणं कुरुते लोकस्तदनुवर्तते॥</p>
<p style="text-align:center;color:var(--muted);font-size:.95em;margin:0 0 16px">yad yad ācarati śreṣṭhas tat tad evetaro janaḥ · sa yat pramāṇaṁ kurute lokas tad anuvartate<br>Bhagavad Gita, Chapter 3, Verse 21</p>

> **Meaning:** "Whatever a great person does, other people also do. Whatever standard they set, the world follows."

Four words carry the verse:

- **ācarati**, *does* or *practises*: Krishna talks about what a person **does**, not what they preach. (The word *ācāra*, conduct, comes from the same root.)
- **śreṣṭhaḥ**, *the best* or *the one looked up to*: at home, that is often a parent, a grandparent or an older brother or sister.
- **pramāṇam**, *the standard* or *the measure*: the example becomes the yardstick others measure themselves by.
- **anuvartate**, *follows*: people walk the path that someone else has already walked.

Krishna does not only say this; he lives it. In the next verses he tells Arjuna that he has nothing left to gain in all the three worlds, and still he keeps working, because if he stopped, people would copy that too. Just before this verse he points to King Janaka, who reached the highest goal through action, and asks Arjuna to act for **lokasaṅgraha**, the good of the whole world.

## Why it matters for children today

The verse works in two directions, and both matter at home:

- **Children watch the grown-ups.** A child who sees a parent read for twenty minutes every evening learns that reading is normal. A child told to "go and read" while the adults scroll their phones learns something else.
- **Children are examples too.** Riya is not a grown-up. She is a big sister, and to Ishaan she is the *śreṣṭha*, the one he looks up to. Every older child in a family, a class or a cricket team is setting a standard for someone smaller.
- **Kind habits spread the same way as careless ones.** Saying "thank you" to the watchman, switching off the fan when you leave the room, putting a wrapper in the bin: one person starts, and others quietly follow.

This is not about being perfect. Everyone forgets, gets tired and makes mistakes. The point of the verse is simpler: **what we do every day speaks louder than what we say once.**

## One small step for today

Today, **pick one good habit and simply do it, without telling anyone.** Line up your shoes. Water a plant. Put your plate in the sink. Say good morning first.

Someone small is watching. Let them see your best.

Families can try it together: each person chooses one quiet habit on Monday and does it every day without announcing it. On Sunday evening, play a guessing game: *"What do you think my habit was? Did anyone start copying it?"*

## FAQ

### Is the story of Riya and Ishaan from the Gita?

No. Riya, Ishaan and the shoes are a simple parable we use to explain the verse. The teaching is Bhagavad Gita 3.21, spoken by Krishna to Arjuna on the battlefield of Kurukshetra.

### What does "yad yad ācarati śreṣṭhaḥ" mean?

It means "whatever the best (or most respected) person does". The full line says that other people do the same, and the world follows the standard that person sets.

### How can I explain this verse to a young child?

Use a mirror game: you move, and the child copies. Then say: "Little ones copy what big people do, just like this. So what should we do, so that they copy something good?"
