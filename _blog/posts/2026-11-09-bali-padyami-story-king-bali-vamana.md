The day after the great new-moon night of Deepavali, many homes in Karnataka welcome a very unusual guest: a king who lost his whole kingdom, and is still loved for it. This is **Bali Padyami** (Bali Pratipada), the day of **King Bali**, and his story is one of the best ways to teach children what a promise is worth.

## The story: a generous king and a tiny visitor

Long ago, the asura king **Bali**, also called **Mahabali**, ruled the earth, the heavens and the worlds below. He was the grandson of **Prahlada**, the boy who never stopped praying to Vishnu. Bali was strong, but he was also famous for one thing: **no one who asked him for a gift went away empty-handed.**

The devas had lost their home in the heavens to Bali, and their mother **Aditi** prayed to Vishnu for help. Vishnu was born to Aditi as a small boy-scholar named **Vamana**. One day, while Bali was holding a great yajna (fire ceremony), Vamana walked in with an umbrella and a water pot.

"What would you like?" asked Bali. "Gold? Cows? A village?"

"Only as much land as I can cover in **three steps**," said Vamana.

Bali laughed kindly. Three tiny steps? Of course. But his guru, **Shukracharya**, warned him: "This is no ordinary boy. Do not promise." Bali answered that a king who goes back on his word is no king at all. He poured water from his pot to seal the gift.

Then Vamana began to grow. He grew taller than the trees, the hills and the clouds, until he became **Trivikrama**, the one of three great strides. With his **first step** he covered the earth. With his **second** he covered the heavens. There was no place left for the third.

Bali folded his hands and bowed his head. "Place your third step here," he said.

Vishnu was moved by Bali's honesty. He gave Bali the beautiful underworld of **Sutala** to rule, and promised to stay with him and protect him. And, as families tell it, Bali was allowed to come back once a year to visit his people. That visit is what Bali Padyami celebrates.

## One line from the oldest book

The three strides of Vishnu are far older than the Puranas. The Rig Veda already sings of them:

> **Meaning:** "Vishnu strode across all this; three times he set down his foot."

## How Bali is welcomed today

- **Karnataka:** Bali Padyami is the fourth day of Deepavali. Many families make a small figure or "fort" of King Bali, decorate the doorstep with rangoli and lamps, and offer prayers to welcome him home. It is also a day for new beginnings in business and in the home.
- **Maharashtra:** the same day is **Bali Pratipada** or Padwa. A well-known wish is <span lang="mr">"इडा पिडा टळो, बळीचे राज्य येवो"</span>, "may troubles go away and Bali's kingdom come", because his rule is remembered as fair and happy.
- **Kerala:** Mahabali's yearly visit is celebrated in a different season, at **Onam**, with flower carpets (pookalam) and a grand feast.
- **North India:** the same day is often **Govardhan Puja**, when Krishna lifting Govardhan Hill is remembered.

Names and customs change from region to region, and the date follows the lunar calendar: it is the first day (pratipada) of the bright half of the month after the Deepavali new moon.

## What children can take from the story

1. **A promise is a gift you give in advance.** Bali kept his even when it cost him everything.
2. **Small can be mighty.** The tiniest visitor at the yajna turned out to be the greatest.
3. **Humility wins hearts.** Bali did not argue or run. He bowed, and that is why he is still loved.

## A five-minute promise activity

1. At dinner, ask each person to make **one small promise** for the week: "I will water the plant every morning," "I will call Ajji on Sunday."
2. Write each promise on a strip of paper and keep them near the family diya.
3. On the next weekend, check together. Celebrate every promise that was kept, and help with the ones that were not.

## FAQ

**Is Bali Padyami part of Deepavali?**
Yes. In Karnataka it is the fourth day of the five-day festival, after Lakshmi Puja on the new-moon night.

**Is Mahabali the same as King Bali?**
Yes. Mahabali means "the great Bali". The same king is remembered at Bali Padyami and at Onam.

**Was Bali a bad king?**
Most retellings remember him as generous and just. The story is less about punishing Bali and more about his honesty and humility.
